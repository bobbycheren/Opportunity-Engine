# Job Search Toolkit

Use the assistance dial for every task here: ask whether they want you to do it, do it together, or coach them through it. Keep using real, current information through web search, and say where it came from.

## 1. Researching careers and pay (the pathway comparator)

When exploring options, build a side-by-side comparison of two to four realistic paths. For each path, find and show:

| Item | Where to look |
|---|---|
| What the work actually is, day to day | O*NET OnLine (onetonline.org), BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook (bls.gov/ooh), and real people in the job |
| Local pay range | BLS occupational wage data for their state or metro area; current local job postings (Indeed, LinkedIn, the state job bank). Show a range, not one number. |
| How many local openings right now | a live search of local postings within their commute range |
| What's required | postings plus O*NET: education, licenses, certifications, experience |
| Training needed, time, and cost | community and technical colleges, apprenticeship.gov, employer-paid training |
| Schedule and conditions | postings; people in the job |
| Where it leads | the next step up and what it pays |

Separate **verified open positions** (a real posting you found, with a link) from **occupational possibilities** (a type of job that exists in the area). Always give the date of the data.

**Show the ladder.** Where it fits, lay out the path in steps with time, cost, and pay at each rung. For example: retail associate ($14/hr) → logistics coordinator ($20–22/hr, about 6 months, employer training) → CDL driver ($25+/hr, 4–8 week CDL course, about $3–6K, often employer-sponsored). Use real local figures you've found, not these illustrations.

**The money picture.** For any move, help them compare take-home pay, benefits (health insurance can be worth thousands a year), commute cost, and childcare. If they receive public benefits, see the benefits rule in SKILL.md: rough estimate only, and confirm with the caseworker.

**State resources.** Look up their state's workforce agency and job bank (e.g., WorkForce West Virginia in WV), its American Job Centers, and community and technical colleges. Many offer free career counseling, free training funding (WIOA), and job listings. Verify names and links with a search; don't rely on memory.

## 2. Moving from a job they dislike to one they'll enjoy

When someone is employed but unhappy:
1. **Diagnose first** (see intake Part 1): is it the work itself, the place, the people, the pay, or feeling stuck? The fix differs: same work at a better employer, a lateral move within the field, or a real career change.
2. **Find the energy clues.** What tasks make time fly? What do they do on a day off by choice? What did they like about past jobs, even bad ones? Look for patterns: people, things, ideas, outdoors, variety, building, helping, solving.
3. **Turn clues into two to four candidate directions**, then test them with real-world experiments (coaching-methods.md §7). Informational interviews are the single most useful tool here.
4. **Search while employed.** It's the stronger position. Keep it discreet: no public "open to work" signal, use personal email and phone, schedule interviews around work, and don't list the current boss as a reference until there's an offer.
5. **Don't quit before the offer** unless they have strong reasons and money to cover the gap. It's their decision, but make sure it's an informed one.
6. **Check fit before accepting**: ask the employer about a typical day, team culture, and why the last person left; look up employee reviews; if possible, talk to someone who works there.

## 3. Resume

Ask first: "Do you want me to draft it, build it together, or review one you write?"

- **Format:** one page for most people (two if they have long, relevant experience). Clean and simple, with no tables, text boxes, or graphics, because screening software (ATS) often can't read them. Standard section headings.
- **Sections:** contact info (city/state, phone, email; no full address needed) → a 2–3 line summary aimed at the target job → skills (matching the posting's words where true) → experience → education and certifications. Add volunteer or community experience if it shows relevant skills.
- **Bullets:** start with an action verb, say what they did and the result, and use numbers when honest ("Trained 6 new hires," "Handled 80+ customer calls a day," "Cut restock time by about a third").
- **Non-traditional experience:** caregiving, household management, and volunteering can go in experience or a separate section, described in work terms. Gaps: be brief and honest ("Family caregiver, 2021–2023"), with no apology.
- **Tailor it** for each job type: match the posting's key requirements and words, truthfully.
- **Produce it** as a downloadable Word document (.docx) they can edit, and keep a master version in mind for later tailoring.

### Building a first resume from scratch

Many people have never had a resume, or have only a little formal work history. Don't start by asking them to "send your work history." Build it through conversation, one section per exchange or two, so it never feels like filling out a form:

1. **Pick the target first.** Ask what kind of jobs they're going to apply to. A resume is aimed at something; if they don't know yet, build a general version for the most likely entry point and tailor it later.
2. **Contact info.** Name, city and state, phone, and an email address that sounds professional. If theirs doesn't (e.g., a nickname or gamer tag), suggest making a simple firstname.lastname address and walk them through it if they want.
3. **Gather raw material by asking, not by asking for a list.** Go one area at a time: any jobs at all (part-time, summer, seasonal, cash or family-business work, babysitting, lawn care, delivery or gig work); school (diploma or GED, year, any classes, programs, vocational or CTE courses, certifications like a food handler card, OSHA-10, CPR, forklift); sports, clubs, church, scouts, military or JROTC; volunteering; things they do for family (caring for siblings or a grandparent, helping with a family business, fixing cars); and skills (tools, equipment, software, languages, a driver's license and a reliable vehicle if relevant). For each one, ask what they actually did, how often, and anything they were trusted with.
4. **Translate into resume language** and show them the translation so they learn how it's done: "Mowed lawns for neighbors" → "Ran a small lawn-care business for 8 regular customers; scheduled jobs, handled payments, and maintained equipment."
5. **Choose the layout.** With little paid work, put a **skills section** and **education** near the top and call the experience section "Experience" (not "Work Experience") so it can include volunteering, informal work, and responsibilities. A short summary line works well: "Dependable, hands-on worker with experience in ___, looking for ___."
6. **Keep it honest and short.** One page, often half to three-quarters full for a first resume, and that's fine. Never pad it with things they didn't do.
7. **Deliver it** as a downloadable Word document (.docx), plus a plain-text version they can paste into online application forms. Explain how to save it on their phone and attach it to an application.
8. **Next steps.** References: help them think of two or three people who aren't family (a teacher, coach, past supervisor, pastor, or neighbor they've worked for) and to ask permission first. Then move on to finding the first few jobs to apply to.

Default to "do it with me" for a first resume unless they ask otherwise: they should come away understanding how it was built, so they can update it themselves later.

## 4. Applications and cover letters

- Help them pick a target number per week that's realistic. Quality beats volume: five tailored applications usually beat twenty generic ones.
- Keep a simple tracker in the plan: employer, job, date applied, link, status, follow-up date.
- **Cover letters** (when asked for or helpful): three short paragraphs. Why this job and this employer specifically; the two or three strongest matches between their experience and the posting, with evidence; a simple close. In their voice, not stiff.
- **Online forms:** they fill them in themselves. You can draft answers to essay or screening questions for them to paste and edit.
- **Follow-up:** a short polite note a week or so after applying, where there's a contact, can help.
- They submit everything themselves. You never claim to have sent anything.

## 5. Networking and informational interviews

For many people, especially career changers, this is the most effective tool and the most dreaded. Make it easy:
- **Who:** friends, family, old coworkers, church or community members, people on LinkedIn in the target job, alumni of their school, local trade associations.
- **The ask:** short, specific, and low-pressure: "I'm thinking about getting into ___ and you're doing it. Could I ask you a few questions for 15 minutes, by phone or over coffee?"
- **Questions to bring:** What's a typical day? What do you like and not like? How did you get in? What would you do if you were starting today? Who else should I talk to? Is anyone hiring?
- **After:** a thank-you message the same day, and a debrief with you (what changed?).
- Offer to draft the message and role-play the conversation if they're nervous.

## 6. Interview preparation

Treat interviewing as a *doing* skill: it improves with practice and feedback.
1. **Research the employer** together: what they do, recent news, the job's main requirements.
2. **Build five to seven stories** from their real experience using a simple structure: the situation, what they did, how it turned out (the STAR method). Cover handling a difficult person, solving a problem, a mistake and what they learned, teamwork, going above and beyond, and why they want this job.
3. **Prepare answers** to the classics: "Tell me about yourself" (60–90 seconds, aimed at this job), "Why do you want to work here?", "Why are you leaving your current job?" (positive and forward-looking, never trashing the current employer), strengths and weaknesses, gaps, pay expectations.
4. **Mock interview.** Play the interviewer. Ask one question at a time, let them answer, then give specific feedback: one thing that worked and one thing to sharpen. Repeat. Offer a "tough interviewer" round if they want it.
5. **Questions for them to ask** the employer (two or three).
6. **Logistics:** what to wear, when to arrive, what to bring, how to handle a phone or video interview.
7. **After:** a thank-you note within 24 hours, then a debrief together: what went well, and what to adjust next time.

## 7. Offers and negotiation

- Help them evaluate the whole offer: pay, hours and schedule stability, benefits (health insurance cost, paid time off, retirement match), commute, growth, and how it fits what they said matters in the intake.
- Compare it with local pay data. If there's room, help them ask for more, politely and with a reason ("Based on my experience with ___ and the local range for this role, I was hoping for ___. Is there flexibility?"). Role-play it.
- If they have multiple offers or are weighing an offer against staying put, lay out the comparison against their "must haves."
- The decision is theirs.

## 8. The first months on the job (retention follow-up)

The moments when people fall back are predictable: the first paycheck gap, a schedule change, a car problem, a conflict with a supervisor, a benefits change. Offer check-ins at roughly **30, 90, 180, and 365 days**:
- How's it going? What's harder than expected?
- Any problems with transportation, childcare, schedule, or a coworker that we should get ahead of?
- What's the next step up, and what would it take? (Restart the ladder.)

Note the check-in dates in the plan so they can come back and you'll know where things stand.
