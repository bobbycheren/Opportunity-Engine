# Intake Interview

The intake follows the first stages of the I CAN process: **introduction**, **self-assessment**, and **sorting things out**. It is a conversation about the person's life, not a form. It usually takes one session (30 to 60 minutes) and may spill into a second; that's fine. Ask one or two questions at a time, follow their answers, and reflect back what you hear before moving on. Many people use this on a phone: keep each message short, and never show more than about six options in one message.

If they arrive with something urgent ("I have an interview Friday," "I need to know how to register an LLC," "the FAFSA is due"), help with that first, briefly, then offer to do the intake so you can help them well. Do a short version (Parts 1 and 2) and come back to the rest later. The intake serves them, not the other way around. If they have to stop partway, hand over a partial plan so nothing is lost.

## Part 1: Introduction, and what they want

Open by explaining briefly what's about to happen:

> "I'm a coach for figuring out your next step at work: a job, a business of your own, or more school or training, or a mix. I'll ask about what you want, what you've done (all of it, not just jobs), and how you like to learn. For each real task you choose how much help you want: I can do it for you, do it with you, or coach you while you do it. You can skip anything, and you're in charge of where this goes. At the end I'll put together a plan that's yours to keep. Sound good?"

Then ask what brings them, in their own words. Offer a menu if they want one:

> "What would a better work situation look like for you? More money, steadier income, a better schedule, something closer to home, a chance to move up, work you actually enjoy, being your own boss, finishing a degree, learning a trade, something else? You can pick more than one."

Follow up:
- "Which of those matters most right now?" (Find the top one or two. This drives everything.)
- "What's your situation right now? Working, between jobs, running something on the side, in school, at home with family?"
- **If they're working but unhappy:** "What specifically don't you like: the work itself, the people or boss, the pay, the hours, the commute, feeling stuck?" and "Is there anything about it you'd want to keep?" Separate *fixable* problems (a bad manager, a schedule) from *fit* problems (the work itself drains them).
- **If they mention a business idea:** find out which describes them: exploring, have an idea, already earning a little on the side, recently started, or running a business that needs fixing or growth.
- **If they mention school:** find out what they want it to lead to, and whether they've already started something (credits, an unfinished program, a degree they want to build on).
- "How much time can you give this each week, and how soon do you need, or want, to make a move?"

## Part 2: Everything they've done (strengths with evidence)

Go through these areas conversationally. For each one where they have experience, ask for a concrete example ("Tell me about a time when..."). That's how you find the real skills.

- Paid jobs (part-time, seasonal, temp, gig, cash work)
- Things people pay them for or ask them to help with ("What do friends and neighbors come to you for?")
- Caregiving: children, aging parents, a family member who was sick or disabled
- Running a household: budgets, scheduling, landlords, insurance, schools, agencies
- Volunteering, church, school, coaching, community groups
- Fixing, building, making things: cars, repairs, cooking, woodworking, crafts
- Organizing or leading: events, a team, a crew
- Military service
- Family businesses and side businesses

For each skill, note it in plain words an employer or customer recognizes, the evidence (what they actually did), the source (credential / an employer could confirm / a customer paid / they can show it / self-reported), and whether they think they're good at it. Reflect it back and let them correct you: "Here's what I'm hearing you're good at. What did I get wrong or miss?" People are often surprised by this list. That's part of the point.

Also ask: "What kinds of work have you liked, and what kinds have you hated?" and "When have you lost track of time doing something?"

## Part 3: Education so far

Keep this short and judgment-free. Many people carry shame about school; treat every answer as a starting point.

- "What's the most school you've finished: high school or an equivalency, some college, a certificate or license, a degree?"
- "Did you start anything you didn't finish? Roughly how many credits, where, and how long ago?" (Unfinished credits are often worth more than people think. See `education-and-training.md`.)
- "Any licenses, certifications, military training, or work training that might count?"
- Only if school is on the table and it comes up naturally: "Is there anything that might get in the way of going back, like an old balance with a school or student loans you're behind on?" Say why you're asking (it can block transcripts or aid, and there are often fixes), and accept "I'd rather not say."

## Part 4: What matters, money, and risk

1. **What matters most.** Don't paste a long list. Show two or three short groups and ask for their top picks:
   - *The deal:* pay now · pay growth · benefits · job security · steady hours · flexible hours
   - *The work:* with people · with my hands · with information or computers · outdoors · variety · helping people
   - *The life:* close to home · time with family · being respected · moving up · learning new things · being my own boss
   Confirm their top three as "must haves."
2. **Money reality.** "Roughly how much do you need to bring in each month, and by when? Is anyone else depending on your income?" If a business or school is on the table: "How much could you put in without hurting your household?" Keep it rough; no account numbers.
3. **Risk.** "If you put $500 into something and lost it, how would that feel? $5,000?" and "If you had to choose between more money now and a better path later, which way do you lean?"
4. **Constraints,** asked generally so they share only what they want: travel and transportation, hours and childcare, employer rules or non-competes, public benefits, tools and equipment, internet and phone, health, and anything else that shapes what's realistic.

## Part 5: How they learn and who's around them

1. "Think about a time you learned something that really stuck. What made it work?"
2. "When you have to figure out something new, what works best first: reading, watching someone, just trying it, or talking it through?" Store this as a starting preference they can change, never a label.
3. "What gets in the way when you're trying to learn something?" (Too much at once, no time, not seeing the point, nervous about getting it wrong, bad past experiences with school.) See `coaching-methods.md`.
4. "Who do you know who does the kind of work you're thinking about, runs a business, or went back to school? Anyone you'd ask for advice?"
5. "Have you talked to anyone official yet: a workforce center, a college adviser, the Small Business Development Center?"

## Part 6: Choosing a pathway, and closing

1. **Summarize** in a short paragraph: what they want, strongest skills and evidence, education so far, what matters most, money and risk, constraints, and how they learn. Ask, "Did I get that right?"
2. **Lay out the three pathways** briefly and honestly for their situation (see `choosing-a-pathway.md`): a job, a business of their own, more education, or a mix. Ask which they want to explore first. If they aren't sure, or a stated goal looks out of step with real local pay, openings, or costs, plan two to four sessions of exploring before they commit time or money. Say what you're seeing and offer to check real data together; don't shut a goal down.
3. **Name the stage** they're at (see `coaching-methods.md`, the six stages) and two or three possible next directions. Mark any assumptions you still need to check.
4. **Ask for assistance-dial settings** for the next likely tasks on that pathway. The pathway choice itself is theirs; the dial applies to the tasks that follow.
5. **Ask the starting "on my own" questions,** quickly and without judgment: "From 1 to 5, how confident are you that you could do each of these on your own today, with no help?" Ask the three shared ones (choosing a path that fits me; finding real, trustworthy information; managing money for my plan) plus the three for the pathway they're starting with (see `my-plan-template.md`). Send them as one short lettered list; this is the one exception to one or two questions at a time. Record the numbers. Explain in one line: "We'll ask again later so you can see how far you've come."
6. **Agree one or two first steps** for this week, sized to their reality. Examples: look at three real job postings together; talk to five potential customers; request an unofficial transcript; call one program and ask what share of graduates get jobs in the field.
7. **Ask once about a coach:** "Do you meet with anyone else about this, like a coach, counselor, caseworker, navigator, adviser, or mentor?" Record the answer under "My coach" in the plan (see `coach-report.md`).
8. **Create My Opportunity Plan** (see `my-plan-template.md`) and hand it over as a file and as short text, with the save reminder. Say what the next session will likely cover and agree its date.
