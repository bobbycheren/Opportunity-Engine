# Choosing a Pathway: Job, Business, or More Education

Read this whenever someone is deciding where to put their time and money, or mixing pathways. The person decides. Your job is an honest comparison with real local figures, a small test before a big commitment, and room to change course.

## The three pathways in one look

| | A job | A business of their own | More education |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best when** | They need steady income soon, want benefits, or want to learn from others | They have evidence people will pay, can live with irregular income, and like running things | A credential would clearly open better work or pay, and they can manage the cost, the time, and the income given up |
| **Main risks** | Low pay, few openings nearby, scams, a bad fit | Irregular income, debt, spending before evidence, benefits or employer conflicts | Debt without a payoff, a program that doesn't lead to work, not finishing, unaccredited schools |
| **Main files** | `job-search-toolkit.md` | `business-modes.md`, `wv-resources.md` | `education-and-training.md` |

## Mixes are common, and often smartest

- **A job now, school later:** earn first, use employer tuition help, start part-time.
- **A job plus a side business:** keep the paycheck and benefits while testing the idea.
- **School while working:** evening, weekend, or online classes; an apprenticeship that pays while they learn.
- **Finishing what they started:** a degree or certificate they're close to completing, while working.
- **A business plus a credential:** a contractor who gets licensed, a baker who takes a food-safety course, a bookkeeper who earns a certification.
- **A credential to change jobs:** a short training program that leads straight into a better-paying job.
- **Waiting and saving first** is a real option, not a failure.

## How to compare, step by step

1. **Name two to four real options** in their words ("CNA at the hospital in town," "mobile detailing on weekends," "finish my bachelor's online," "the welding certificate at the community college").
2. **Collect real figures for each** with a web search and, where needed, local asking:
   - Income: pay in current local postings; prices local customers actually pay; what the credential leads to, from real postings and the program's own outcomes. Source and date each figure.
   - Costs: commute, childcare (including sick days and odd shifts), tools, materials, startup costs, tuition and fees, books, and the income given up while studying or building.
   - Time: when money starts coming in, how long a program takes, how long a business takes to break even.
   - What it opens next: the next step up and what it pays.
3. **Do the money picture** for each: money in minus money out per month, and how many months until it pays off. A program with free tuition can still cost a year of lost wages; a job that pays more can leave less after a longer commute.
4. **Weigh what matters to them** (their top three from the intake) and their family duties.
5. **Name the riskiest assumption** for each option ("Hospitals near me are hiring CNAs," "People will pay $60 a car," "A degree will get me promoted").
6. **Design a small test** for the favorite (see below), then decide.

Put the comparison in the plan's "Paths I'm comparing" table. In chat, give each path in two or three short lines unless they ask for a table.

## Small tests before big commitments

| Pathway | Example tests |
|---|---|
| Job | Talk to two people doing the work; shadow a shift; apply to five real postings and see what comes back |
| Business | Ask ten potential customers; take three paid orders; offer the service for two weekends with a small spending cap |
| Education | Talk to the program and to recent graduates; ask the program's job placement rate and typical pay; sit in on a class; take one class or a free module first; get credits evaluated before enrolling |

Set a spending cap and a deadline, and ask them to set what success looks like. After the test, review honestly: continue, adjust, or switch.

## Changing course

Plans change. If a test fails or life shifts, record what was learned in the plan and compare again. Name what carries over: a resume built for a job search helps with college applications; costing skills from a business help with any budget; credits earned years ago may still count; a license helps in both a job and a business.
