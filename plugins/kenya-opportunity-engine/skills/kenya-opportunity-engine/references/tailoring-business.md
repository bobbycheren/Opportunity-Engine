# Running a Tailoring Business in Kenya

Written October 2026 for graduates of PFC sewing schools and anyone sewing for money. Prices in this file are indicative and dated. Local prices vary widely between Nairobi, towns, rural areas, and Kakuma. **Build every price from the person's own costs and their local market.**

## 1. What to make: common product lines

| Line | Why it can work | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Repairs and alterations (zips, hems, taking in, patching) | Little money needed; steady demand, including from mtumba (second-hand clothes) buyers who need fitting | Low price per job; needs a busy spot |
| School uniforms | Big, predictable demand before term opens, especially January | Schools may favour a set supplier; fabric money needed up front; colours and specs must match exactly |
| Made-to-order outfits (kitenge and African print, dresses, shirts, skirts) | Higher value; repeat customers and referrals | Fitting skill matters; customers may bring their own fabric |
| Church, choir, and group uniforms | Bulk orders from one decision-maker | Deadlines, deposits, and exact matching |
| Occasion wear (weddings, graduations, holidays) | High value | Seasonal; mistakes are costly; agree designs in writing or with a photo |
| Bags, aprons, and small items | Can be made in batches and sold at markets or online | Needs a selling channel; compete on design and quality |
| Home items (curtains, cushion covers, table cloths) | Larger orders, measured jobs | Measure carefully; agree fabric and lining |
| Sanitary pads (reusable) and face masks | Sometimes NGO or school demand | Hygiene standards may apply; check before selling |

Help them choose one or two lines to start, based on evidence of demand, the money they have, and their skill.

| **Price** | cost + profit, checked against what others charge |

Rules of thumb to teach:
- **Their time is a cost.** A price that only covers fabric is a loss. To set an hourly figure, start from what they earn per hour in other work, or a local day wage divided by the hours, then adjust.
- **Customer brings fabric:** the price is for labour and materials like thread and zips only. Agree it before cutting.
- **Compare with the market.** Ask three to five tailors or shops what they charge. If their cost is higher than the market price, look at faster methods, cheaper materials, a different product, or a different customer.
- **Bulk orders:** a lower price per piece can work if the time per piece falls, but check that it still covers costs.

## 3. Deposits and orders

- Ask for a **deposit** before cutting fabric for made-to-order work. Many tailors ask for about half, or for the cost of the fabric. Help them decide their own rule and say it confidently.
- Write each order down: customer name and phone, item, measurements, fabric (theirs or the customer's), price, deposit paid, balance, and the promised date. A photo of the design helps avoid disputes.
- Agree what happens if a customer doesn't collect (for example, after a set number of weeks the tailor may sell it to recover costs). Say it at the start, kindly.
- Keep customer fabric safe and labelled.
- **Selling on credit** is the most common way small tailors lose money. Help them set a rule, practise saying it, and keep a list of who owes what.

## 4. Machines and equipment

Indicative online prices, mid-October 2026 (single listings on Kenyan sites; brand names on listing sites are often loose). Always check locally and inspect before buying:
- New manual (hand or treadle) domestic machine, such as Butterfly: about KSh 6,500–16,000.
- Industrial straight-stitch machine, complete set: about KSh 48,000–65,000.
- Overlocker: about KSh 10,500 and up, depending on type.

Advice to give:
- **Manual and treadle machines** work without electricity and are cheaper to buy and fix. Electric and industrial machines are faster but need reliable power.
- **Start with what earns.** One good machine plus scissors, tape measure, chalk, iron, and needles is enough to start. An overlocker can wait until orders show it's needed, or they can pay another tailor to overlock. Check payback first: if they pay others KSh 20 a piece for 40 pieces a month (KSh 800), a KSh 10,500 overlocker takes about 13 months to pay for itself, before repairs.
- **Second-hand machines:** test it in person (sew a seam, check the stitch, listen for grinding), ask for a receipt, and be careful of stolen goods.
- **Shared or group machines** can be the best start.
- Never pay in full for a machine from a stranger online before seeing it.

## 5. Fabric and supplies

- Buy close to home when possible; larger markets (for example Gikomba, Eastleigh, and Kamukunji in Nairobi, or the main market in their county town) may be cheaper but cost fare and time. Include the fare in the cost sheet.
- Buy in amounts matched to confirmed orders until the business is steady.
- Keep receipts. If they ever need to claim business expenses for tax, KRA requires electronic (eTIMS) invoices from suppliers; see `kenya-business-rules.md`.

## 6. The calendar

- **School uniforms:** demand peaks before terms open, especially in January. Plan fabric money ahead.
- **December and religious holidays:** occasion wear, new outfits, and travel.
- **Wedding and graduation seasons** vary by area.
- **Slow months:** use them for samples, skills, and finding bulk buyers.

## 7. Finding customers

- **Word of mouth** is still the strongest channel: neighbours, church or mosque, chama members, schools, and former classmates. Ask happy customers to tell others.
- **WhatsApp:** a business profile, a catalogue or status updates with photos of real work (with the customer's permission if they're in the photo).
- **Facebook and TikTok** reach many people in Kenya, but views don't always turn into buyers. Free posting first; don't pay for adverts until they know what sells.
- **Market days** and a visible spot with a sign.
- **Bulk buyers:** schools, churches, choirs, women's groups, NGOs, hotels, and small companies. Help them prepare a short, polite introduction and a price list.

## 8. Tenders and bigger contracts

- Government buyers reserve a share of contracts for youth, women, and persons with disabilities through **AGPO** (see `kenya-business-rules.md`). It requires a registered business and a tax compliance certificate. Large uniform contracts (police, national institutions) usually go to textile mills; small tailors more often win small lots, school and county orders, or subcontracting.
- **No one can sell a tender.** Apply only through the official portal (agpo.go.ke) or a Huduma Centre, check there whether any official fee applies, and pay only through official channels. Anyone asking for money to "register you faster" or "secure" a tender is a scammer.
- Before bidding, model the cash needed for fabric up front and the time to deliver. A tender that can't be financed or delivered on time can damage a small business.

## 9. Skills and certificates

- Their PFC sewing certificate is evidence of training. Customers judge mostly by the work itself, so photos of finished garments are a strong portfolio.
- A **NITA Government Trade Test (Grade III)** in dressmaking or garment making is a low-cost, nationally recognised credential that helps with schools, institutions, tenders, and jobs. Short courses (pattern making, industrial machines, embroidery, bags) and diplomas are covered in `education-and-training.md`.

## 10. Health and safety

- Good light, regular breaks, and a proper chair reduce strain.
- Keep scissors, needles, and irons safe around children.
- If using a generator, never run it indoors.
