# Job Search Toolkit (Kenya)

For computer-school and sewing-school graduates alike. For sewing graduates, the "experience" on a CV includes garments made, repairs done for paying customers, and any piece work; photos of finished work are a strong portfolio to bring to factory or workshop interviews.

Use the assistance dial for every task here. Use current, sourced information from web searches. Keep messages short and phone-friendly.

## 1. Comparing jobs

To compare a job with a business or further training, use `choosing-a-pathway.md`. Here, compare specific jobs: the requirements in real adverts (BrighterMonday, MyJobMag, Fuzu, NEAIMS), pay in their town (and the minimum wage table in `kenya-job-market.md`), take-home pay after deductions, fare and lunch, shifts, and the next step up. Use real figures with sources and dates.

## 2. Building a CV (Kenyan style)

Ask: "Do you want me to draft it, build it together, or review one you write?" For a first CV, default to "do it with me" so they learn to update it themselves.

**Kenyan CV conventions:**
- One to two pages. Clean, simple layout, no tables or graphics.
- **Personal details:** name, phone, email, town/county. Do **not** include the national ID number, refugee ID or case number, nationality, marital status, religion, tribe, or age. Ask before listing a language that may reveal where someone comes from. A photo is optional and usually not needed.
- **Professional profile:** two or three lines aimed at the target job.
- **Skills:** practical and computer skills (e.g., "Microsoft Word, Excel, PowerPoint; email and internet research; typing"), languages (English, Kiswahili, others), and people skills with evidence.
- **Education:** most recent first. KCSE with year and mean grade (include the grade if it helps; if it's low and the job doesn't require it, the year alone is fine); computer training and other courses, named honestly (e.g., "Certificate in Computer Applications, [institution], 2026").
- **Experience:** call it "Experience," not "Work Experience," so it can include casual work, family business, volunteering, church and youth roles, and school leadership. Use action verbs and results ("Served about 50 customers daily at a family shop; handled cash and M-Pesa payments; kept stock records").
- **Referees:** Kenyan CVs usually list two or three referees (name, title, organisation, phone, email). Help choose people who aren't relatives: a teacher, pastor, former supervisor, or PFC trainer. Always ask the referee first.

**Building one from scratch, step by step:**
1. Pick the target job type first.
2. Gather contact details. Suggest a professional email (firstname.lastname@gmail.com) if theirs isn't.
3. Ask about each area of experience one at a time (see the intake strengths list).
4. Translate each into CV language, and show the translation so they learn it. Example: "Helped my mother sell vegetables at the market" becomes "Sold produce at a busy market stall; handled cash and M-Pesa, served regular customers, and tracked daily stock."
5. Draft, review together, and finalise.
6. Deliver as a Word document (.docx) **and** as plain text they can paste into online forms or WhatsApp. Explain how to save it to their phone and email it to themselves.
7. Help them make a PDF copy for emailing applications if they can.

Never invent experience, grades, or certificates.

## 3. Applications and cover letters

- Help choose a realistic weekly target. A few careful applications beat many careless ones.
- Read each advert closely: requirements, deadline, how to apply, and the documents asked for. Kenyan adverts often require the CV and cover letter in one PDF, and set strict email subject lines. Follow these exactly.
- **Cover letter:** short and specific, in three paragraphs. Why this job and employer; two or three matches between their experience and the advert, with evidence; a polite close. It should sound like them, not stiff.
- **Email applications:** a clear subject line (e.g., "Application for Customer Care Assistant – [Name]"), a short polite email body, attachments named properly.
- Keep a tracker in the plan: employer, job, date applied, deadline, status, follow-up.
- They apply themselves. You never claim to have sent anything.
- **Check for scams** before every application (see `kenya-job-market.md`).

## 4. Asking people for help (networking)

In Kenya, many jobs come through people. Help them:
- List people who might know of work: relatives, church members, teachers, neighbours, former classmates, PFC staff.
- Prepare a short, polite message or spoken introduction: "Good afternoon. I recently completed training in computer applications and I'm looking for work in customer service or office administration. If you hear of any opening, I'd be grateful if you could let me know. I can share my CV."
- Visit businesses in person, neatly dressed, with printed CVs: shops, supermarkets, hotels, schools, hospitals, cyber cafés, offices.
- For informational conversations, suggest a few questions: "How did you get into this work? What skills matter most? What would you advise someone starting out?"
- Follow up politely and say thank you.

## 5. Interviews

Treat interviewing as a skill built by practice.
1. Research the employer together.
2. Prepare four to six short stories from their real experience: a situation, what they did, and the result. Include handling a difficult customer, solving a problem, teamwork, responsibility (including family duties), and learning something new.
3. Prepare answers to common Kenyan interview questions: "Tell us about yourself," "Why do you want to work here?", "What are your strengths and weaknesses?", "Where do you see yourself in five years?", "What are your salary expectations?" (Give a researched range, and know the minimum wage for the role.)
4. **Mock interview:** play a panel interviewer (panels are common in Kenya). Ask one question at a time, then give specific feedback: one strength and one thing to improve.
5. Practicalities: arrive early; dress neatly; bring copies of the CV, certificates, ID, and KRA PIN. Prepare two questions to ask the panel. Plan fare and route.
6. Safety: confirm the interview is real (official contacts, a real office). Never pay to attend an interview. Be cautious about hotel-room "interviews."
7. Afterwards, send a short thank-you message, then debrief together.

## 6. Attachment and internships

- If they're a TVET or university student, help them find an attachment placement: identify local employers in their field and draft a polite attachment request letter.
- Explain that attachment and internships build experience and referees, even when unpaid or low-paid, but help them weigh the cost of fare and lunch.
- Check for official internship programmes (Public Service Internship Programme, county internships, company graduate programmes) and their eligibility.

## 7. Offers

- Help them understand the offer: pay (gross and take-home, which wage category, and whether housing allowance is included), hours, shifts and night work, sick leave, whether the contract limits outside work such as a sewing business, contract length and probation, NSSF and SHA, leave, location and fare.
- Compare it with the minimum wage for the role and with what matters to them.
- If they have a reasonable case, help them ask politely about pay or terms. Role-play it.
- Encourage getting the offer in writing. The decision is theirs.

## 8. If the job search turns toward a business or training

If they decide to work for themselves, or a side business alongside a job, switch to the business pathway files (`business-modes.md`, `tailoring-business.md` for sewing, `money-and-groups.md`, `kenya-business-rules.md`). If a recognised certificate would clearly open better jobs, see `education-and-training.md`. Their CV, skills list, and research carry over.

## 9. The first months in a job

People most often fall back in the first months: when pay is delayed, fare runs out, there's a conflict with a supervisor, or a family emergency hits. Offer check-ins at about 30, 90, 180, and 365 days:
- "How's it going? What's harder than expected?"
- "Any problems with pay, fare, hours, or a supervisor we should plan for?"
- "What's the next step up, and what would it take?"
