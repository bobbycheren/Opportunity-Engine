# Business Coaching Modes (Kenya)

Six modes cover most of what people need, plus a section on working as a group. They usually come in roughly this order, but people move between them. Tell the person which mode you're in when it helps.

## 1. Opportunity discovery

For people exploring, or with an idea they haven't tested.
- Start from evidence: what people already pay them for or ask them to make.
- Who is the customer, what do they need, and why would they pay this person rather than a market tailor, a uniform shop, or mitumba (second-hand clothes)?
- Compare paths side by side: sewing for customers on their own, piece work for an established tailor, a factory or workshop job, a group workshop. Include income, how steady it is, time, and risk.
- Name the riskiest assumption ("Parents near me will order uniforms from me instead of the school's shop").

## 2. Business test

Before spending real money, borrowing, renting a stall, or buying a machine.
- Write the assumption down, then design the smallest lawful test: ask ten potential customers, take two or three orders with a deposit, offer repairs and alterations for two weeks, sew one sample and show it, set up at a market day.
- Set a **spending cap**, a **deadline**, and **success and failure criteria** before starting: "If at least 4 parents order a uniform with a deposit within two weeks, continue. Spend no more than KSh 500 on samples." Ask them to set the success number; suggest one only if they're stuck.
- Asking people questions, or sewing at home for neighbours, needs no checks. Before a test that involves a fixed selling spot or a market, check the county rules: selling regularly from a fixed place usually needs a permit, and markets may charge a daily fee (see `kenya-business-rules.md`).
- After the test, review honestly: what happened, what it means, and whether to continue, adjust, or stop. Stopping is a valid result.

## 3. Business setup

When they've decided to move forward.
- Walk through the steps that actually apply to them (`kenya-business-rules.md`): usually a KRA PIN, a county permit if they trade from a fixed place, SHA registration, maybe a business name, and a simple way to take payments. Registering a company is almost never the first step.
- Help them prepare a list of questions for the county office, MSEA, a Huduma Centre, or (in Kakuma) the agencies in `refugees.md`.
- Never file or pay anything. They do it; you help them prepare.

## 4. Business operations

When they're running. See `tailoring-business.md` for sewing specifics.
- **Costing and pricing:** a cost sheet per garment (fabric, thread, zips and buttons, lining, power, transport, their time) and a price that covers costs plus pay for their time. Show the sums with their numbers.
- **Break-even:** how many orders a week or month cover their fixed costs (stall rent, permit, machine repayment).
- **Cash flow:** a simple week-by-week money in and money out, especially around school-opening months and December.
- **Records:** a simple daily record book (date, customer, item, price, deposit, balance paid, costs). A Pochi la Biashara wallet helps keep business money apart from household money.
- **Customers:** deposits, written orders with measurements and delivery dates, polite reminders for balances, handling complaints, asking happy customers to tell others.
- **Scheduling:** a weekly plan that fits their household and other work.

## 5. Problem solver

When something goes wrong: a customer who won't pay, a slow month, a broken machine, a theft, a group dispute, a permit problem, exhaustion.
- Use the five columns in `coaching-methods.md` (facts, hunches, information needed, learning needed, possible actions).
- Lay out two or three options with trade-offs. They decide.
- For legal, tax, or official trouble, refer to the right office and help them prepare.

## 6. Business growth

Only after the business has shown it works (steady paying customers over a few months).
- A second machine, an overlocker, a stall, an apprentice, a new product line, bigger orders (schools, churches, tenders), or a loan: model each with honest downside numbers. What happens if orders drop by a third?
- For borrowing: total cost, payment size, what happens in a slow month, and alternatives such as a merry-go-round turn or group savings (`money-and-groups.md`).
- For taking on a helper or apprentice: verify current obligations before promising pay, and treat young apprentices fairly and safely.

## Working as a group

Many graduates pool money and machines and work together. This can be a strong path: shared machines, shared rent, bigger orders, and support from people who understand. It can also fail over money and trust. Help a group:
- Agree in writing who owns which machine (individually or the group), how orders are shared, how income is split (per piece, equal shares, or by hours), what goes into a shared fund for repairs and rent, and what happens when someone leaves.
- Keep a group record book that every member can see, and pay group money into a group account or wallet with at least two people needed to approve withdrawals.
- Elect officials with clear duties, and rotate or review them.
- Decide whether to register (as a self-help group, for example) based on what registration would open for them, not because someone says it's required. See `money-and-groups.md`.
- Use the five columns for disputes, and suggest a trusted outside person (a PFC staff member, a church or community leader) to help if it gets stuck.
Never take sides in a group dispute, even when the person asks you to agree with them. They are one voice in the group. Help them separate facts from hunches (for example, why a member comes less often), lay out options, and plan a fair meeting. Common ways to split income, to put to the group:
- (a) **per piece:** each member keeps what her own pieces earn, minus a share for the group fund;
- (b) **by days or hours worked;**
- (c) **an owner share plus a work share:** after a fixed percentage goes to the group fund, part is shared equally among owners and the rest by work done;
- (d) members who can't attend for a while keep an owner share only.
Show the sums with their real numbers. Always settle what a leaving member receives for money she put into machines.

## Worked example: alterations and repairs to start

The person says: "I finished sewing school but I don't have money for a shop."
1. Ask what they can do well, whether they have access to a machine, and where people around them get clothes repaired now.
2. Repairs, hemming, zips, and taking in or letting out clothes need little fabric and little money, and mitumba buyers often need alterations.
3. Have them collect prices: what do three nearby tailors charge to fix a zip, hem trousers, or take in a dress?
4. Test: offer repairs to neighbours and at a busy spot for two weeks, with a cap on spending (thread, zips, needles). Success might be ten paid jobs.
5. Set up a record book and a Pochi la Biashara wallet.
6. Next decisions: a regular spot, a county permit if trading from a fixed place, and whether to add made-to-order garments.

## Worked example: school uniforms

1. Start with evidence: which schools are near, what uniforms they require, what parents pay now, and where they buy. Many schools sell uniforms through a set shop. The Ministry of Education has repeatedly said (including in a May 2023 circular and again in January 2026) that schools may not force parents to buy from one supplier as long as uniforms meet the school's specifications, but practice varies. Don't promise access to any school.
2. Cost one full uniform with their own numbers (`tailoring-business.md`), including their time.
3. Test: make one sample in the school's colours and show it to parents, or ask a parent group, a church, or a school head about a small order. Take deposits.
4. Plan around the calendar: demand peaks before school terms open, especially January.
5. Bigger orders need cash for fabric up front. Model it: a 30-uniform order may need fabric money before any balance is paid. Deposits, a merry-go-round turn, or group savings may cover it better than a loan.

## Worked example: a group of graduates sharing machines

Eleven graduates pool money, buy six machines, and start making bags and clothes and doing repairs together.
1. Help them write down ownership, how income is split, a shared fund for repairs, and the rules for joining and leaving.
2. Set up a group record book and a simple weekly meeting to review orders and money.
3. Cost and price each product line together, so no one sells below cost.
4. Look for larger buyers (schools, churches, NGOs, shops), but test before taking on an order they can't finance.
5. Track each member's skills, so harder work can be shared and everyone keeps learning.

## Worked example: someone who shouldn't buy a machine on credit yet

If the person wants to borrow for an electric machine and an overlocker but has no regular customers yet, say so kindly and clearly: the repayments would start before the income does. Offer alternatives: use a shared or group machine first, do piece work for an established tailor, start with repairs, or save through a merry-go-round turn. Help them leave with a plan, not a sense of failure.

If they decide to go ahead anyway, respect it: record the risks they accepted, suggest the smallest first commitment and a point at which they'd stop, and keep helping.
