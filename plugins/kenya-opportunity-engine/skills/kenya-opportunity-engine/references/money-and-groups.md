# Money, Groups, Loans, and Grants (Kenya)

Written October 2026. Loan terms, fees, and grant windows change often. **Confirm any figure on the official site or with the provider before the person relies on it**, and give the date and source when you quote one. Never recommend a specific lender or loan. Your job is to help them understand the options, do the sums with their real numbers, and decide.

Order of advice for most new businesses: **savings and group money first, grants if one is genuinely open, borrowing last and small.**

---

## 1. Merry-go-rounds (mzunguko)

Merry-go-rounds are one of the most important money tools a sewing graduate has. Take them seriously, explain them well, and help people use them safely.

### How they work

Every member pays the same amount at each meeting (for example KSh 200 or 500 a week), and one member takes the whole pot. When every member has had a turn, the cycle ends; the group may start again. No interest is paid and nothing stays in the group between payouts. The payout order is usually set at the start by drawing numbers, by agreement or need, or by swapping turns with the group's consent.

Variants:
- **Goods rotations:** the pot buys an item for one member each time (household goods, a gas cylinder, solar panels). Groups in Kenya do this. A tailors' group could rotate machines or bulk fabric so each member gets one in turn, though that particular use isn't documented and needs careful pricing (see the worked example).
- **One shared item is not a merry-go-round.** If a group wants ONE thing for the workshop (one overlocker, one stock of fabric), that is **group target saving**: divide the price by the members (KSh 12,000 ÷ 11 is about KSh 1,091 each), choose a weekly amount everyone can manage, and count the weeks. Always ask first: "One for the group, or one for each member?" Agree in writing who owns the shared item and what a member who leaves gets back.
- **Merry-go-round plus savings or lending:** many chamas run a rotating pot alongside a savings or loan pool.
- Related but different: **table banking / ASCAs / village savings and loan associations (VSLAs)**, where savings build up into a fund members can borrow from with interest that returns to the group; **investment chamas**, which pool money to buy assets; **SACCOs**, which are formal cooperatives.

### How common they are

About 3 in 10 Kenyan adults (around 8.6 million people) used informal groups such as chamas and merry-go-rounds in 2024, slightly more than in 2021, and use is higher in rural areas (FinAccess Household Survey 2024, CBK/KNBS/FSD Kenya). Women rely on informal savings much more than men. In earlier Kenyan financial diaries research, most households saved in a merry-go-round at some point, and merry-go-rounds were the most widely used informal tool for buying things like livestock, roofing, and paying school fees.

### Why they work (what the research says)

- **Commitment.** In a well-known Kenyan study, members explained simply: "you can't save alone." The meeting, the group, and the fixed day make saving happen when it otherwise wouldn't (Gugerty 2007).
- **A lump sum from small payments.** A merry-go-round turns weekly small amounts into one usable sum, which is how many people buy something that can't be bought in pieces, like a machine.
- **Social support.** Members encourage and check on each other. In a western Kenya experiment, a savings pot run inside existing merry-go-rounds helped people save for health costs far more than a simple savings box did, especially people who found it hard to save on their own (Dupas and Robinson, 2013).
- Research in Nairobi also found women use merry-go-rounds partly to protect savings from household demands (Anderson and Baland, 2002). Share this only as research if it comes up; never advise anyone to hide money from family.

### The limits and risks

- **Early and late turns are different deals.** An early turn is like an interest-free loan from the group: you receive the pot before you've paid most of it. A late turn is pure savings: you've lent your money to the others, and you carry the most risk.
- **The biggest risk is a member who stops paying after receiving the pot.** Then every later pot is short. Treasurers disappearing with cash, and fake account records, are also documented in Kenya, including in a garment workers' chama.
- **Not emergency money.** Money paid in can't be taken out early, so keep a separate small emergency fund or use a savings group that lends.
- **Payouts can be too small to change a business.** Match the contribution to the goal (see the example).
- Inflation matters much less than default and theft: over a 10-week cycle the value lost by the last member is tiny compared with the risk of an unpaid turn.

### How good groups protect their members

Help a person check or set up these protections:
1. **Know the members.** Join groups of people you know or who are vouched for by someone you trust. New or unknown members take later turns.
2. **Written rules**, agreed and signed: amount, day, payout order, late fees, what happens if someone misses, how disputes are settled, and exit rules (a member who has received the pot must keep paying to the end; a member who leaves before receiving gets her contributions back at the end of the cycle). Agree what happens when a member can't pay one week (catch up later, a swap, or a smaller amount). Where income is very irregular, as in Kakuma since the 2025 food aid cuts, set the amount the lowest-earning member can manage, or contribute a share of sales instead of a fixed sum.
3. **Fixed order in writing**, with swaps only by agreement and recorded in the minutes.
4. **A guarantor or deposit** for early-turn members, if the group wants extra safety.
5. **Records everyone can see:** a record book or app, meeting minutes, and receipts.
6. **No large cash held by one person.** Use a group account or wallet that needs two or three officials to approve withdrawals, and let members see statements themselves, not just hear about them.
7. **Meet regularly.** Suspended meetings and "just send it to my number" are warning signs.

### Choosing a turn and choosing a group

- Ask for an **early turn only if you're sure you can keep paying every week to the end.** Using an early turn to buy a machine that earns money can be smart, but the payments still come every week.
- If the group is new to you, a **later turn** is safer for the group and builds trust.
- **Turn your turn into a date.** Ask the secretary which week you receive, put the date in the plan, and compare it with when the business needs cash (for example, fabric money before January uniform orders).
- **Say what the pot will actually buy** at local prices: fabric for how many uniforms, or how much of a machine.
- **Swaps and selling a turn** happen only with the group's agreement and should be written in the record book.
- **Join a merry-go-round** when you need a lump sum for something specific, find it hard to save alone, and know the members.
- **Save alone** (M-Pesa savings, a bank or SACCO account) when the members are strangers, the group is online only, or your income is too irregular for a fixed day.
- **Join a VSLA or table-banking group** when you need emergency loans, flexible borrowing, or some return on savings.

### Worked example: ten tailors, KSh 500 a week

The pot is KSh 5,000 a week and the cycle lasts 10 weeks.
- Member 1 receives KSh 5,000 in week 1 after paying KSh 500. She owes the group KSh 4,500 more in payments: an interest-free loan.
- Members 5 and 6 are lenders to the group for about half the cycle and borrowers for the other half.
- Over the full cycle, every member gets back exactly what she paid in. The difference between turns is timing and risk, not money.
- Member 10 receives KSh 5,000 in week 10 after paying KSh 5,000. She has lent the group up to KSh 4,500 along the way and is exposed to every earlier member's default.
- If member 1 disappears after week 1, every later pot is KSh 500 short unless the rules or a guarantor cover it.

**Buying machines:** a single KSh 5,000 pot won't buy most machines. Options: raise contributions (ten members at KSh 1,300 a week gives a KSh 13,000 pot, enough for a basic manual machine at October 2026 online prices, but check local prices), combine the pot with personal savings, or use the pot for fabric and supplies instead. Always do the sums with the group's real figures, and check that every member can afford the weekly amount for the whole cycle.

### A short rules sheet the coach can draft with them

1. Members: [names], [number] in total. New members only if two members vouch for them.
2. Amount: KSh ___ every [day], paid by M-Pesa to [group account or wallet] or at the meeting, recorded in the book.
3. Order: written below, drawn by lots on [date]. Swaps only with group agreement, written in the book.
4. Late payment: KSh ___ fine after [time]. Missing two payments in a row means [rule].
5. A member who has received must keep paying to the end. A member who leaves before receiving gets her money back at the end of the cycle.
6. Money: no member holds more than one meeting's cash; withdrawals need [two or three] officials; every member can see the records.
7. Disputes: discussed at the meeting first; if not settled, ask [a trusted outside person].

### Scams that look like merry-go-rounds

- **No genuine merry-go-round pays out more than members put in.** Any group that promises to multiply money, or pays you for recruiting others (often called "blessing," "gifting," or "loom" schemes), is a pyramid scheme, and most people in it lose. Report it to the police (DCI).
- Be very careful with WhatsApp, Telegram, or Facebook "chamas" run by strangers, including "appliance" or "school fees" chamas. Many admins have disappeared with members' money.
- Never send contributions to a personal number you can't check, and never share M-Pesa PINs.

### Registration and tax

- A simple merry-go-round **doesn't have to register.** Registering as a self-help group (State Department for Social Protection, via the sub-county social development office or Huduma Centre; KSh 1,000 for a group as of October 2026) can open doors: some group bank accounts, Safaricom's M-Pesa Chama Account, and government group loans such as Uwezo, which require registration.
- The Community Groups Registration Act 2022 was declared unconstitutional by the High Court in September 2026, with the declaration suspended until about February 2027 to let Parliament fix it. The rules may change, so check before registering.
- Some banks open accounts for informal groups with minutes naming signatories (for example Co-op Bank's Mshiriki account and I&M, as of 2026). Check current terms.
- KRA has said merry-go-round contributions are savings, not income, but chamas that lend at interest or invest may owe tax, and a chama with a KRA PIN must file returns. For anything beyond a simple merry-go-round, tell them to check with KRA.

## 2. Other groups and savings

- **Table banking / VSLAs:** common in Kakuma and rural areas; members save weekly, borrow from the fund, and repay with interest that stays in the group. Often run with a three-key box opened only in front of everyone.
- **SACCOs:** check that a deposit-taking SACCO is licensed by SASRA (the list is on sasra.go.ke). Unlicensed SACCOs are riskier.
- **M-Pesa and bank savings:** M-Shwari lock savings, bank accounts, and similar. Useful for an emergency fund.

## 3. M-Pesa for business

- **Pochi la Biashara:** a business wallet inside M-Pesa, opened from *334# with no paperwork. It keeps business money separate from household money, and customers pay the fee (Safaricom reduced these fees under a promotion that runs to 31 October 2026; after that date, assume the standard charges until confirmed). NYOTA has paid grants into Pochi wallets.
- **Lipa na M-Pesa Buy Goods (Till):** the merchant pays a fee per transaction; opening one usually needs ID, KRA PIN, a county business permit, and a bank account. Better once the business is established.
- Encourage one simple habit: all business money goes into Pochi, and the person pays themselves a set amount from it.

## 5. Loans and mobile credit

- **Show the real cost** with their offer's figures: total repaid, payment size and timing, and what happens in a slow week.
- **Fuliza and daily-fee overdrafts** become very expensive when rolled over, because of daily fees on small balances. Use them only for a day or two, if at all.
- **Digital lenders:** only use lenders licensed by the Central Bank of Kenya (the directory is on centralbank.go.ke). Unlicensed apps often harass borrowers and their contacts.
- **Credit records (CRB):** lenders may not report debts under KSh 1,000 to credit bureaus, and licensed digital lenders must give 30 days' notice before reporting. A bad listing can block future loans.
- **Bank and microfinance loans** for small businesses often need a permit, records, and a guarantor or collateral. In Kakuma, bank loans have been around 20% interest; some business-support organisations lend at lower rates (see `refugees.md`).
- Never borrow to pay another loan without a plan, and never borrow for a business that hasn't yet shown paying customers.

## 6. Money scams to warn about

- Fees to apply for grants, funds, tenders, AGPO, NYOTA, or "NGO support."
- People offering to "help" with a Hustler or NYOTA application for a fee, or asking for a PIN.
- Fake M-Pesa "reversal" messages and "wrong number" payment requests.
- Pyramid schemes and money-multiplying merry-go-rounds.
- Offers to buy a machine or starter kit with guaranteed orders.
- Anyone asking to borrow your ID or SIM.

If they've lost money: screenshot everything, call Safaricom (or their network) about the transaction right away, and report to the police (DCI).
